Limitations (please read)

  • SPHEREx has only about one year of public data so far: slow objects move less than 2 pixels.
  • Pixels are 6.2 arcsec; parallax (< 0.6 arcsec) is far too small to see.
  • Very bright stars can saturate; saturated pixels are counted and their brightness is not reliable.
  • SPHEREx wavelength changes across each detector, so brightness between epochs is only approximate.
  • This is an exploration tool: a moving dot is a candidate to check, not a discovery.
  • A new position (your own coordinates) is computed live and needs the NASA IRSA archive to be online. The five example targets are pre-computed and open even when IRSA is down.

How to read it

Flipbook: each frame is the median of a few SPHEREx images taken within days of each other (same detector), aligned on the sky. Stars stay put; anything that moves jumps between frames.

Difference: each epoch minus the first. Grey = no change, white = brighter now, black = fainter now. A moving star shows as a white and black pair.

Link: every view has a URL such as ?ra=269.45&dec=4.69&pmra=-801.6&pmdec=10362&fov=0.1 that you can share or embed.

Learn more: the ideas behind it
What is SPHEREx?
A NASA space telescope launched in 2025. It scans the whole sky in 102 infrared “colours” from 0.75 to 5 micron, and repeats the survey several times.
Why does every image have its own wavelength?
Each detector sits behind a filter whose colour changes smoothly across it (a linear variable filter). In each exposure the star lands on a different part of the filter, so each image sees it at a different wavelength. Putting many images together gives a spectrum. This is also why the same star can look brighter in one image than in another without being variable.
Proper motion
Stars drift slowly across the sky. Barnard's Star moves 10.4 arcsec per year, the most of any star, which is about 1.7 SPHEREx pixels per year.
Brown dwarfs
Objects between a planet and a star, too light to burn hydrogen. Epsilon Indi B is a T-type brown dwarf; WISE 0855 is only about 250 K (about −23 °C), so it shines almost only in the infrared, beyond 4 micron.
Variable stars
RW Aurigae is a young star that dims irregularly; R Draconis is a Mira variable that swells and shrinks over about 245 days.
Saturation
A very bright star fills pixels beyond what the detector can count, so its centre is lost. We still use its position but not its brightness.
Signal-to-noise (S/N) and mJy
S/N of 5 or more means the signal is at least five times the usual noise: normally a real detection. 1 mJy (millijansky) is a unit for the brightness of a source.
